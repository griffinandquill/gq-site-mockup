How it works

The same three moves show up in a tutoring session, a lesson, and a street in Quillhaven.

  1. 1

    Play

    Step into the question

    A source, a map, a choice, or a scene with something at stake. The student looks first, before anyone explains the answer.

  2. 2

    Learn

    Learn the hard part

    A South Carolina teacher walks through the hard part: the term, the disagreement, or the sentence that will not land on the page.

  3. 3

    Prove

    Say it back

    The student states a claim, points at the evidence, and says why it holds. That is the legend they get to keep.

Ryan Mayer teaches 11th-grade U.S. History at Battery Creek High School in South Carolina. His social studies courses are U.S. History and the Constitution (USHC), Economics, AP Human Geography, and Government. He has taught social studies for more than seven years and holds a Master’s in History Education.

Griffin & Quill LLC is his South Carolina company: tutoring, Teachers Pay Teachers curriculum, and South Carolina history browser games. The classroom habit is the same one the company asks for: read the source, weigh the claim, and write the answer in your own words.

The work is meant to sit beside the standards he teaches. When a card names a standard, that standard is marked as a sample until the real one is confirmed.
